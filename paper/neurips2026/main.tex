\begin{abstract}
Pivotal Token Search (PTS) labels a generated token as pivotal when it
moves the model's probability of reaching a correct answer past a
threshold, estimated by sampling many rollouts and adjudicating each with
an oracle. We ask whether that expensive, outcome-grounded property is
already present in the model's activations \emph{one position before} the
token is emitted --- the only position at which a prediction could still
gate an intervention. We generate PTS events for three model scales
(Qwen3-0.6B, 1.7B, 4B) over 3{,}000 GSM8K questions each, using a
reimplementation of the search that batches rollouts across queries and
emits exact token positions, and train linear probes at $t{-}1$.
Pivotality is decodable at every scale, and the probe beats every cheaper
explanation we tested --- token identity, next-token entropy, top-2 margin,
top-1 probability --- with differences bootstrapped over questions on paired
resamples. The informative pattern is how the margin \emph{scales}: probe
AUROC rises monotonically with model size ($0.807 \to 0.863 \to 0.897$)
while entropy stays flat ($0.693 \to 0.721 \to 0.694$), so the advantage
over predictive uncertainty widens from $+0.112$ ($p{=}0.013$) to
$+0.204$ ($p{<}0.001$). Larger models encode pivotality more linearly, and
what they encode is increasingly \emph{not} uncertainty. We also show the
comparison is fragile in a way that is easy to miss: drawing negative
examples from the whole rollout rather than the span where pivots occur
inflates entropy to 0.814 and erases the advantage entirely. Moving the
probe to the pivot token itself inverts entropy to \emph{below chance},
replicated across scales --- uncertainty peaks immediately before a pivotal
token and collapses immediately after. We further find that the \emph{sign} of
the impending shift --- whether the next token will help or hurt --- is
itself decodable at $t{-}1$, but only at scale: a clean null at 0.6B
(separated from nothing, not even a random direction) becoming 0.780 AUROC
at 4B, separated from every cheaper explanation. Because entropy is
sign-blind, and because a lookup on the current token cannot encode the
sign of a token not yet emitted, this result is immune to both deflationary
accounts. We also document two ways such measurements inflate: collapsing a question's sampled rollouts to one
canonical sequence discards 74\% of the supervision, and selecting the best
layer on the reporting split adds 0.063 AUROC.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A language model solving a multi-step problem does not distribute its risk
evenly across the tokens it emits. The Phi-4 technical report
\citep{abdin2024phi4technicalreport} formalized this as \emph{Pivotal Token
Search} (PTS): a token $t$ is pivotal when appending it to a prefix moves
the estimated probability of eventually solving the task past a threshold,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pts}
  |\pdelta| = \bigl| P(\text{success} \mid \text{prefix} + t)
              - P(\text{success} \mid \text{prefix}) \bigr| > \tau .
\end{equation}
Estimating either term requires sampling many completions and adjudicating
each with an oracle, so PTS is an offline instrument that costs tens of
rollouts per candidate position.

Our starting question was whether that instrument can be amortized: is
pivotality linearly decodable from the residual stream at $t-1$, before the
token is produced? A probe read one step early is a different object from
one read at the pivotal token itself---the latter is a post-hoc
classifier, the former is available while there is still a decision to
influence.

It is. But the more informative result is \emph{what else} predicts it.
The question is only interesting if the answer is not already known for a
cheaper reason, and two cheaper reasons exist. \emph{Token identity}: some
tokens may simply be pivotal more often, in which case a lookup table
suffices and no representation is implicated. \emph{Next-token
uncertainty}: high-entropy ``forking'' tokens are the incumbent notion of
a critical position \citep{wang2025beyond8020}.

The probe clears both, but the margin over uncertainty is small enough
that how the comparison is set up decides the answer. We show that an
unexamined choice about where negative examples are drawn from can erase
it entirely, and we report the comparison both ways.

Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Evidence that pivotality is linearly decodable at $t-1$ at three
    model scales, above token identity and every next-token uncertainty
    statistic, using a paired bootstrap over questions
    (Section~\ref{sec:main-result}).
  \item The scaling result: the probe improves with model size while
    uncertainty does not, so the advantage widens from $+0.112$ to
    $+0.204$ (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}).
  \item A demonstration that this comparison is fragile --- sampling
    negatives from the whole rollout rather than the pivot-bearing span
    erases it (Section~\ref{sec:negatives}).
  \item A contrast between reading at $t-1$ and at the pivot, in which
    entropy inverts to below chance at all three scales
    (Section~\ref{sec:at-t}).
  \item The signed result: whether the impending shift is helpful or
    harmful is decodable at $t-1$ at 4B (0.780) though not at 0.6B, and is
    immune to both the uncertainty and token-identity accounts
    (Section~\ref{sec:signed}).
  \item A characterization of two ways such headlines inflate: branch
    collapse and layer selection (Sections~\ref{sec:labels} and
    \ref{sec:layers}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Pivotal tokens.} \citet{abdin2024phi4technicalreport} introduce
PTS to build preference pairs for Phi-4 post-training; \citet{pts}
released an open implementation and event datasets. Both use PTS as a
data-generation step; the representational question---whether the property
is present in activations beforehand---has not to our knowledge been
asked, nor has the relationship between PTS labels and uncertainty been
measured.

\paragraph{Uncertainty as the incumbent account.}
\citet{wang2025beyond8020} show that roughly 20\% of tokens carry high
entropy, act as branch points, and account for most of the gain from
reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Sections~\ref{sec:main-result}
and \ref{sec:negatives} measure how much of PTS's criterion that account
already covers, and how sensitive the measurement is.

\paragraph{Sentence-scale reasoning importance.} Thought Anchors
\citep{bogdan2025thoughtanchors} identifies influential sentences by
counterfactual resampling. We work at token scale, where the $t-1$
prediction problem is well posed: a sentence boundary is not a decoding
step.

\paragraph{Probing and steering.} Linear probes on intermediate
representations \citep{alain2018understandingintermediatelayersusing,
marks2024geometryoftruth}; contrastive activation addition
\citep{rimsky2024caa,huang2024steering}; conditional steering
\citep{lee2024conditionalactivationsteering}. Process reward models and
value probes \citep{li2024qprobe} estimate
$P(\text{success} \mid \text{prefix})$, a \emph{level}; pivotality is
$|\pdelta|$, a \emph{sensitivity}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Generating the labels}
\label{sec:labels}

We reimplement token-scale PTS with the same semantics as \citet{pts} but
express the bisection as a state machine, so that the rollouts of many
questions can be batched together. The tree is sequential within a
question---both children of a node need the node's own midpoint
estimate---but questions are independent, and batching across them gives
$8.9\times$ the generation throughput of running one node at a time.
Emitted events carry the pivotal token's exact absolute position and the
full searched rollout.

Two construction details materially affect the result.

\paragraph{Branches, not a canonical sequence.} PTS samples several
rollouts per question and they diverge after a few tokens. A construction
that collapses a question to its longest rollout and matches the others
against it as string prefixes silently discards every pivot on a diverging
branch. On the released Qwen3-0.6B events, 104 questions carry 1{,}245
pivotal tokens over 447 distinct branches, and collapsing retains 318 of
them---a 74\% loss. We treat every maximal branch as its own sequence, and
because branches share prefixes (and the residual stream at position $p$
depends only on tokens $0\ldots p$) we label each distinct context exactly
once.

\paragraph{Positions are exact, not inferred.} Byte-pair encoding does not
guarantee $\mathrm{enc}(a\,{+}\,b) = \mathrm{enc}(a)\,{+}\,\mathrm{enc}(b)$,
so recovering a pivot's index by re-tokenizing its prefix is unsafe: a
pivot token such as ``.'' frequently merges with the preceding word. For
data we generate this problem does not arise. For the released datasets,
which predate the position field, we verify each recovered index against
the reported token id and discard rows that fail.

\subsection{Label noise in the oracle}
\label{sec:labelnoise}

$\widehat{\pdelta}$ is a difference of two binomial estimates from $S$
rollouts each, accepted when $|\widehat{\pdelta}| \ge \tau$. Under the null
of no true change $\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\pdelta}) = 2p(1-p)/S$, so at the
reference setting $S{=}50$, $\tau{=}0.2$ the acceptance threshold sits at
only $2\sigma$: a false-acceptance rate near 4.6\% per tested position at
$p{=}0.5$. We therefore store the raw counts
$(n_{\text{before}}, s_{\text{before}}, n_{\text{after}}, s_{\text{after}})$
rather than a verdict, which makes $\tau$ a post-hoc knob and lets every
event carry a Wilson interval. \TODO{report the accuracy gap between the
full label set and a high-confidence subset.}

\subsection{Probes, baselines, and how they are compared}
\label{sec:probes}

Probes are $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regressions on the residual
stream, fit on standardized features and converted back to raw activation
space so the resulting vector is usable as a direction. Layer and
regularization strength are chosen on an inner split of the \emph{training}
questions; the test split is used once.

Baselines: token identity, as a smoothed log-odds lookup and as one-hot
logistic regression, both fit on train and scored on held-out rows;
next-token entropy, negative top-2 margin, and negative top-1 probability,
computed from the model's own distribution at $t-1$; and a random unit
direction.

\paragraph{Comparisons are paired.} Confidence intervals are bootstrapped
over \emph{questions}, since positions within one reasoning trace are not
independent. Crucially, when comparing the probe against a baseline we
bootstrap the \emph{difference} of AUROCs on the same resampled questions,
rather than checking whether two marginal intervals overlap. The latter is
badly conservative and would, on our data, have supported a conclusion the
paired test rejects.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Models: Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B \citep{qwen3technicalreport}
(28, 28 and 36 decoder layers; $d = 1024$, 2048, 2560). Task: GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, conditioned on the bare question without a chat
template, matching the released PTS datasets and therefore without
extended-thinking mode. Search: 3{,}000 candidate questions per model,
$S{=}40$ rollouts per probability estimate, one searched rollout per
question, $\tau{=}0.2$, 320 max new tokens, on a single Blackwell-class
GPU per model.

PTS only visits questions whose baseline success probability lies inside
$[0.2, 0.8]$: a question the model always or never solves has no pivotal
token, because a saturated probability cannot move. Table~\ref{tab:data}
summarizes what each search produced. Notably the qualifying fraction
\emph{rises} with model size rather than falling --- at 320 new tokens with
raw conditioning, none of these models is close to saturating GSM8K.

\subsection{The advantage over uncertainty grows with scale}
\label{sec:scaling}

The single most informative pattern in Table~\ref{tab:main} is not any one
number but a trend. Probe AUROC rises monotonically with model size,
$0.807 \to 0.863 \to 0.897$, while next-token entropy stays essentially
flat, $0.693 \to 0.721 \to 0.694$. The gap therefore widens from $+0.112$
($p{=}0.013$) at 0.6B to $+0.204$ ($p{<}0.001$) at 4B.

This rules out the most deflationary reading of a single-scale result. If
larger models were simply ``easier to probe'' in some generic way, the
cheap detectors would improve alongside the probe; they do not. What
improves is specifically the linear availability of pivotality in the
residual stream, and what it is pulling away from is specifically
predictive uncertainty.

The margin over \emph{token identity} does not show the same trend
($+0.110 \to +0.149 \to +0.107$): token identity itself gets stronger with
scale (0.696 $\to$ 0.715 $\to$ 0.791), so probe and lookup table improve
together. The widening is uncertainty-specific.

\subsection{Where the negatives come from decides the comparison}
\label{sec:negatives}

That comparison depends on a choice which is easy to leave implicit. Our
rollouts run to the token limit, and the natural thing is to sample
non-pivotal positions uniformly across the stored sequence. Doing so draws
most of them from the fluent tail, \emph{after} the model has committed to
an answer: on Qwen3-0.6B, 63\% of negatives landed beyond the last pivot,
with mean next-token entropy 0.275 against 0.476 before it, and median
position 232 against 146 for pivots.

Under that sampling entropy rises from 0.693 to 0.814 and the probe's
advantage over it falls to $+0.039$ with an interval spanning zero --- a
null result manufactured entirely by the negative distribution. We
therefore restrict negatives to the span in which pivots occur, and we
recommend that any comparison of this kind state where its negatives came
from. Published PTS datasets avoid the problem incidentally, because the
sequence they store stops at the deepest pivot; it appears as soon as one
keeps the full rollout, which is otherwise the more useful artifact.

\subsection{The sign of the impending shift is decodable, at scale}
\label{sec:signed}

Pivotality as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:pts} is symmetric: a token that
rescues an answer and one that ruins it receive the same label. The sharper
question is whether the \emph{sign} is decodable at $t-1$ --- whether the
model represents, one step early, that it is about to make a mistake.

This question is immune to Section~\ref{sec:negatives} by construction.
Entropy is sign-blind: it cannot distinguish a helpful branch point from a
harmful one, and indeed scores \emph{below} chance on this task at every
scale (0.514, 0.428, 0.434). Whatever a signed probe reads, it is not
predictive uncertainty.

0.6B alone, gave 0.717 and looked promising; it did not survive a cleaner
dataset. The result reported here is the opposite case --- weak where the
data is weak, and strong only where scale supports it.

\paragraph{Why $t-1$ is the honest position for this question.}
Reading at the pivot instead raises the signed probe to 0.697, 0.859 and
0.890. But most of that is not representation: token identity rises from
0.56--0.70 at $t-1$ to 0.74--0.85 at $t$, because at the pivot the token
being classified \emph{is} the pivot, and helpful and harmful tokens differ
lexically. At 0.6B the lookup table actually beats the probe there (0.735
versus 0.697). At $t-1$ that escape route is closed by construction --- a
lookup on the current token cannot encode the sign of a token that has not
been emitted --- so the $t-1$ column is the one that isolates representation
from vocabulary.

\subsection{What the probe amortizes}
\label{sec:amortization}

PTS is an instrument, not a mechanism, and it is an expensive one. Isolating
the pivots in a single searched query cost a measured $14.1$ / $15.8$ /
$14.4$ bisection midpoints at 0.6B / 1.7B / 4B, each of which is $S{=}40$
rollouts of up to $320$ tokens: roughly $1.8$--$2.0 \times 10^{5}$ generated
tokens per query. At the usual $2N$ floating-point operations per generated
token, that is $2.2 \times 10^{14}$, $6.9 \times 10^{14}$ and $1.5 \times
10^{15}$ operations respectively.

The probe's judgment is one dot product per position. Over the same
$320$-position rollout that is $2dT$ operations --- $6.6 \times 10^{5}$ at
0.6B, $1.3 \times 10^{6}$ at 1.7B, $1.6 \times 10^{6}$ at 4B. The ratio is
$3.3 \times 10^{8}$, $5.2 \times 10^{8}$ and $9.0 \times 10^{8}$: between
eight and nine orders of magnitude, widening with scale because the search
cost grows with $N$ while the probe's grows with $d$.

Two qualifications, both of which matter more than the ratio. The probe
reads the residual stream of a forward pass that generation is performing
anyway, so this is a \emph{marginal} cost --- the comparison is fair for a
monitor running alongside decoding, and unfair as a claim about replacing
PTS offline. And the substitution is lossy: at 0.807--0.897 AUROC the probe
approximates the oracle's verdict rather than reproducing it. The honest
statement is that a judgment which costs a fifth of a million generated
tokens to make exactly can be approximated, imperfectly, for free during
decoding --- which is what makes gating an intervention on it conceivable
at all.

\subsection{No layer is special, and selection inflates the headline}
\label{sec:layers}

On Qwen3-0.6B, per-layer accuracy spans 0.651--0.741 with mean 0.702.
Simulating a null in which all 29 layers are equally informative, each
measured on 232 rows, gives an expected maximum of 0.762 against an
observed 0.741. There is no evidence of layer specialization; the signal is
spread across the mid-to-late stack, and naming a single ``pivotal layer''
would be an artifact of maximizing over correlated noise. Consistently, the
selected layer sits at 76--79\% of depth in all three models
($L{=}22/29$, $23/29$, $24/37$) without any of them being distinguishable
from its neighbours.

The cost of getting the protocol wrong is measurable. Choosing the layer on
training data and reporting on test gives 0.807; taking the best layer on
the reporting split gives 0.814. Under a leakier protocol we measured the
same gap at 0.063 --- large enough to manufacture a result.

\subsection{Regularization}
\label{sec:reg}

On Qwen3-0.6B, AUROC moves from 0.752 at $C{=}1$ to 0.816 at $C{=}10^{-3}$,
and the probe direction rotates toward the mean-difference direction as the
penalty tightens (cosine 0.173 to 0.765). A weakly regularized probe still
classifies, but along a substantially different direction --- which matters
for anyone intending to use the weights as an intervention direction rather
than only as a classifier.

\section{Causal validation}
\label{sec:causal}

A decodable direction need not be a used one. This section asks whether
gating an intervention on the probe changes what the model answers, and it
is written to be falsifiable: the primary arm, its coefficient, and its
firing rate are fixed before any arm runs, and every other arm is reported
as a control or as a curve.

\paragraph{What the probe supplies that the detector does not.} PTS labels
a token pivotal when $|\pdelta| > \tau$. That criterion is
sign-symmetric, so the direction it induces has no good end: ``more
pivotal'' means ``the next token matters more,'' which is as likely to
amplify a mistake as to prevent one. The intervention direction is
therefore the \emph{signed} one, $\mu_{\text{helpful}} -
\mu_{\text{harmful}}$, estimated on training questions only. At 0.6B and
1.7B this direction is close to orthogonal to the unsigned one (cosine
$-0.05$ and $-0.12$); at 4B they are mildly aligned ($+0.22$). The gate is
the cascade $p_{\text{steer}} = P(\text{pivotal}) \cdot (1 -
P(\text{helpful}))$: fire when the next token is likely to be a pivot
\emph{and} likely to be a harmful one.

\paragraph{Where the intervention is applied.} Both probes and the
perturbation act on $\text{hidden\_states}[L]$ at a single layer $L$,
through a forward hook on the module whose output is that tensor. Sharing
one layer is not a convenience: a gate read above the steering site could
not influence the token it fired on. $L$ is chosen on an inner split of
training questions to maximize $\min(\text{AUROC}_{\text{unsigned}},
\text{AUROC}_{\text{signed}})$ --- a cascade is only as good as its weaker
half. This selects layer 11 at 0.6B, 25 at 1.7B and 21 at 4B; the latter
two coincide with the layers the signed-probe evaluation of
Section~\ref{sec:signed} chose under a different objective.

\paragraph{The evaluator had to be rebuilt first.} Our earlier GSM8K
harness measured 0.16--0.20 for Qwen3-0.6B against a published
$\approx 0.60$, and was confounded on four independent axes: an instruct
model scored without its chat template, a 256-token budget that truncated
correct answers mid-sentence, an answer parser reading the full decoded
sequence so that predictions could be lifted out of the question, and one
RNG seed per arm consumed across variable-length generations, so that base
and steered arms were genuinely paired only for the first example. Each
moves accuracy without touching reasoning quality, and activation
perturbations change generation length --- exactly the quantity the
confounds respond to. The rebuilt evaluator applies the chat template,
parses the response only, instructs the answer format, budgets 640 tokens,
and decodes greedily so that pairing is exact. It recovers
\textbf{0.567 / 0.817 / 0.940} at 0.6B / 1.7B / 4B.

\paragraph{Evaluating in the band where pivots exist.} That last number is
the problem. At 0.940 there are eighteen questions left to move, and 4B is
the only scale at which the signed probe separates from token identity. We
therefore evaluate on the acceptance band PTS itself searches: questions
whose success probability lies in $[0.2, 0.8]$. This is the population
pivotality is defined on, since a token cannot shift $P(\text{success})$
for a question whose outcome is already settled. The band is estimated
from its own independent sampled rollouts, before any arm runs ---
selecting on whether the \emph{base arm} answered a question correctly
would condition on a random outcome, and regression to the mean would then
flatter any intervention.

\paragraph{Controls.} Four, each removing a different explanation. An
\textbf{always-on} arm at matched injected energy asks whether the gate
matters or only the perturbation budget; this is the comparison the
selectivity claim rests on. A \textbf{random-placement} arm at matched duty
cycle, same direction and coefficient, separates \emph{where} the
intervention fires from \emph{how much} of it there is. An
\textbf{unsigned-direction} arm tests whether polarity matters. A
\textbf{sign-flipped} arm negates the coefficient: a real effect must
reverse. In our earlier runs, coefficients $+0.96$ and $-1.04$ had
identical $\|\delta\|$ and both scored above baseline, which is what a
noise floor looks like rather than a dose-response.

\paragraph{Thresholds are calibrated, not guessed.} A probability
threshold is not a firing rate. An observe pass runs the gate without
perturbing --- so its generations are identical to the baseline's --- and
the threshold for a target rate is read off the resulting quantiles over
decode positions. A separate identity check confirms that the hook at
coefficient zero reproduces the baseline exactly, which separates the
plumbing from the perturbation.

\subsection{Why we read this as a null rather than a small effect}
\label{sec:nullreading}

Three pieces of evidence, none of which depends on a $p$-value.

\paragraph{Negating the coefficient never hurts.} At all three scales the
sign-flipped arm matches or beats the primary: $+0.018$ against $+0.007$,
$-0.013$ against $-0.013$, $+0.009$ against $-0.015$. A direction with
causal polarity cannot behave this way. The earlier round of this project
saw the same signature --- coefficients $+0.96$ and $-1.04$ with identical
$\|\delta\|$, both above baseline --- and read it as a dose-response.

\paragraph{Disjoint halves of the same population disagree in sign.} GSM8K
train and test index different questions, so the 4B band was evaluated as
two runs. They point opposite ways on every arm:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
arm & test half ($n{=}99$) & train half ($n{=}237$) \\
\midrule
reactive (primary) & $+0.030$ & $-0.034$ \\
always-on, matched energy & $+0.071$ & $-0.025$ \\
coefficient negated & $+0.040$ & $-0.004$ \\
random placement & $+0.000$ & $-0.017$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Same model, same probe, same layer, same coefficient, two disjoint samples
from one uncertainty band. Reported alone, the test half is a rising
dose-response with a $+0.071$ always-on arm; the train half is a monotone
degradation. Both are noise, and only running both makes that visible.
This was not a designed replication --- it fell out of train and test
indexing differently --- but it is the most useful thing in the section,
because it measures the noise floor on the same axis as the effect.

\paragraph{The dose-response is not monotone where it matters.} At 4B,
$\alpha \in \{0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$ gives
$\{-0.015, -0.015, +0.009, -0.009\}$: no ordering. At 1.7B it \emph{is}
ordered, $\{+0.000, -0.013, -0.040, -0.054\}$, and monotonically
\emph{downward}, as is the fire-rate sweep
$\{+0.027, -0.013, -0.047\}$. Where a dose-response exists at all it
describes damage accumulating with perturbation size, independent of
direction --- consistent with the destructive regime reported for
always-on additive steering, on a gentler slope because the gate fires on
5\% of positions.

\paragraph{What this does and does not establish.} It does not show that
pivotality is causally inert. It shows that this intervention --- a
single-position additive nudge along a mean-difference direction, at
$\|\delta\| \le 0.2\|h\|$, gated at 2--10\% duty --- does not move GSM8K
accuracy, on 336 questions, with a resolution of roughly $\pm 0.02$. A
real effect smaller than that would be invisible here. Three obvious
directions remain untested: perturbing a span rather than a position (our
hysteresis arm reached only 16\% duty), acting on attention or MLP
outputs rather than the residual stream, and steering toward a
\emph{target} representation rather than along a difference of means. We
report the null because the experiment was pre-registered and the
controls are unambiguous, not because we think the question is settled.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

One model family on one task family: three scales of Qwen3 on GSM8K, with
91, 104 and 130 held-out questions. The oracle's labels are themselves
noisy (Section~\ref{sec:labelnoise}) in a way we have quantified
analytically but not yet empirically. The
acceptance filter retains only mid-difficulty questions, so pivot density
is confounded with task difficulty relative to model capability and this
label set is not a uniform sample of GSM8K. Conditioning on the bare
question puts an instruct model off distribution; we observed rollouts in
which the model hallucinated multiple-choice options, and at least one
event whose extreme $\pdelta$ traces to the answer-extraction cascade
matching such a hallucination rather than to a genuine shift. Linear
probes test linear decodability only.

The causal experiment carries its own limits, stated in
Section~\ref{sec:nullreading}: 336 questions at 4B give a resolution of
roughly $\pm 0.02$ accuracy, so a genuine effect below that would not be
detectable, and we tested one intervention family rather than the space of
them. The probes are also applied off their fitting distribution --- they
are fit on raw-conditioned PTS rollouts and run during chat-templated
generation. That shift did not degrade the gate ($P(\text{pivotal})$ has
mean 0.30 and standard deviation 0.19 over 121{,}016 decode positions at
4B, not a degenerate distribution), but it is a gap between where the
probe is measured and where it is used.

At the smallest scale the margins over uncertainty are $+0.10$ to $+0.11$
with lower bounds within 0.01 of zero, on 91 questions. They are
separations, not comfortable ones, and Section~\ref{sec:negatives} shows
how little it takes to erase them; the case rests on the trend across
scales rather than on any single interval.

\section{Conclusion}

Pivotality is linearly present in a model's residual stream one position
before the token that realizes it, at 0.807 to 0.897 AUROC across three
model scales, above token identity and above every next-token uncertainty
statistic we tested. The advantage over uncertainty widens with scale while
uncertainty itself stays flat, which is the strongest evidence we have that
the probe reads something other than local predictive difficulty. Reading
at the pivot instead inverts entropy to below chance at every scale,
locating the uncertainty signal specifically at $t-1$ --- the one position
that is both informative and available while a decision can still be
influenced. The \emph{direction} of the impending shift is
decodable too, but only at scale: a null at 0.6B and 0.780 AUROC at 4B,
separated from token identity, from uncertainty (which is sign-blind and
scores below chance), and from a random direction. That the signed signal
is closely approximated by a contrastive mean-difference direction makes it
the natural object for a causal test.

That test is negative. Gating an additive intervention along
$\mu_{\text{helpful}} - \mu_{\text{harmful}}$ on the probe changes GSM8K
accuracy by $-0.015$ at 4B on 336 questions in the uncertainty band, with
every control indistinguishable from it, and the same null at both smaller
scales. The detector is not what fails: at the layer the intervention acts
on, the cascade identifies harmful pivots at 0.836 AUROC. Reading and
acting on a representation are different things, and this work establishes
the first without the second. The negative result is sharper than a
missing experiment would be, because it isolates where the gap lies, and
because the controls --- a sign-flipped arm that never underperforms the
primary, and two disjoint halves of one population that disagree in sign
on every arm --- make the size of the noise floor explicit rather than
leaving it to be inferred.
